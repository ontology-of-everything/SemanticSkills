## Abstract (summary)

The paper challenges a core principle of object orientation: that a system's
functionality can be partitioned amongst objects corresponding to individuals
in the problem domain. Jackson argues this partitioning is neither natural nor
straightforward, and that the fragmentation and conflation of functionality
that later software-engineering developments tried to mitigate were in fact
inevitable consequences of this founding principle. He proposes abandoning
that principle and replacing it with an approach that decouples problem-domain
individuals from the modules that partition functionality.

## 1. Historical Preamble (summary)

Traces the lineage from Jean-Raymond Abrial's 1974 *Data Semantics* paper,
which modeled system state as global relations over object identities —
bridging early object orientation (Simula 67) and Codd's relational model, and
laying the foundation for Chen's entity-relationship model. Notably, Abrial
did **not** adopt object-oriented structuring; in Z and later B he continued
to view state relationally. Jackson observes that the modularity mechanisms
developed for Z/B were aimed mainly at easing specification and verification,
missing an opportunity: the paper reuses classical formal-methods ideas
(relational state, events as state predicates, event synchronization) to
organize software with better separation of concerns. He notes precursors:
the 1987 CAVIAR reservation-system specification (with a generic parameterized
reservation module), whose modularity ideas trace back to Abrial's original
1981 version, and a proposal for event synchronization in Event-B that yields
a language very similar to the one described here.

## 2. Introduction (summary)

Starting from the 1991 Business Week cover promising that objects would make
software "simple," Jackson argues objects turned out to be neither simple nor
self-contained. Despite object orientation's conventional status as a success
story, code remains hard to write, read, and change; developers still entangle
code with couplings ("dependency hell"). LLM-based coding follows the same
practices and threatens to amass technical debt at scale. Jackson contends
many contemporary problems are direct, inevitable consequences of object
orientation itself. Object orientation is a complex of distinct ideas that
became conflated; one particular principle — combining two influential ideas —
causes much of the trouble and can be dropped without abandoning OO
programming entirely, provided it is replaced by a principle that achieves the
same goals without the liabilities.

## 3. Object orientation: a complex of ideas (summary)

Sketches the distinct strands that merged into "object orientation":

- **Tiny machines** — systems as collections of communicating agents (Simula
  67, Hewitt's actor model, Kay's objects). For Kay, late binding meant
  message sending was communication, not delegation — in contrast to
  Dijkstra/Hoare's view of calls as delegation of responsibility.
- **The WIMP interface** — contextual command menus attached to icons; in
  Smalltalk icons were literally objects, but the correspondence is hard to
  maintain (e.g., deleting a file mutates a directory entry, not the file).
- **Abstract data types** — Parnas's decomposition by encapsulated data
  structures; Liskov & Zilles's data viewed purely via operations;
  representation independence and information hiding;
  "responsibility-driven design" as the practitioner term.
- **Domain modeling** — placing a model of the problem domain at the center of
  the program (Michael Jackson's JSD as an early example; precursor to OMT and
  domain-driven design, including event storming). Distinctive claim: *all*
  systems, even real-time control, benefit from this treatment.
- **Object identity** — from Abrial's data semantics: entities need
  persistent identities to map consistently to the world; contrasts with the
  relational model's lack of identity (normal-form theory partly compensating
  for that lack). Abrial's objects have no internal structure — attributes
  attach via global relations, not containment.
- **Objects as records** — objects as records of fields, then (per William
  Cook's definitional move) records containing only functions over hidden
  state; from Sketchpad through Simula 67 and Smalltalk.
- **Classes and inheritance** — neither is needed for object-based dispatch;
  inheritance has fallen out of favor due to modularity violations and single
  inheritance's inflexibility.

## 4. The Roots of Trouble: Combining Two Ideas (summary)

Two ideas jointly cause the trouble:

1. **Behaviors are obtained by assigning computations to objects** (the "tiny
   machines" strand; Meyer's *Single Target* principle — every operation is
   relative to a current instance).
2. **Objects correspond directly to entities in the problem domain** (the
   domain-modeling strand; Booch's definition of object-oriented analysis).

Combined, they imply each computation should be assigned to a single object
representing a domain entity. The usual escape hatch — allowing "design
objects" distinct from "analysis/domain objects" — does not solve the problem,
because almost no computation can be satisfactorily assigned to a domain
object; the programmer chooses an arbitrary assignment, and legibility and
modularity suffer.

## 5. Modeling Behavior (summary)

Constructs an independent account of behavior using a restaurant-reservation
example. Phenomena come in three kinds:

- **Individuals** — participants with distinct, persistent identity (the term
  "individual" is preferred over object/entity).
- **Relationships** — facts associating individuals with each other or with
  simple values; all observable properties are captured in relationships.
- **Actions** — atomic occurrences that involve individuals and change
  relationships; activities with duration are modeled by start/end actions.

Example phenomena: users Alice and Bob; restaurants Maido and Rosetta; slots;
reservations; relations such as `for`, `by`, `time`, `date`, `restaurant`;
actions such as `reserve(Alice, Slot_3):(Reservation_1)` and
`cancel(Reservation_1)`.

A **trace** is a sequence of actions; at each point a set of relationships
holds; trace plus relationships comprise a **behavior**. Valid behaviors are
defined by initial relationships plus, per action type, a precondition and a
postcondition (which mutually constrains before-state, after-state, arguments
and results). The paper gives an informal spec of `reserve(u: User, s: Slot):
(r: Reservation)` — requiring no existing reservation for the slot and
ensuring a new reservation for `s` by `u` — and notes that action
specifications are cast in general terms over *types* of phenomena.

## 6. Object-oriented Modularization (summary)

A modularization assigns each phenomenon type (individual, relation, action)
to one or more modules — not necessarily a partition — with a closure rule:
including an action requires including the individuals and relations in its
specification. Object-oriented modularization then means: each individual type
corresponds to a class, and there is one module per class. Relations follow
the actions that mention them (mirroring the rule that methods access only
their object's instance variables). The scheme is elegantly parsimonious —
individual types double as modules — and the task reduces to assigning each
action type to an individual type. But this turns out to be much harder than
one might imagine.

## 7. Assigning Actions to Individuals (summary)

- `reserve (User, Slot): (Reservation)` involves three individual types; which
  gets the action? Assigning by "who acts" (User) collapses the
  modularization; Meyer's motto ("Ask what it does it to!") suggests Slot; but
  if any single assignment is to work, it must be Reservation — because only a
  class's constructor can allocate its instances.
- `cancel (Reservation)` involves one individual — yet if canceled
  reservations are simply discarded, the action *cannot* live in Reservation:
  in modern OO languages a class cannot deallocate its own objects; the object
  must be disconnected from those pointing to it (suggesting Slot with the
  `for` relation, or User with the `by` relation). An action about a single
  individual must be assigned to a different individual.
- Adding a precondition that a user not hold another reservation at the same
  time and restaurant forces the question of where that state lives. Assigning
  it to Restaurant makes Restaurant a container of reservations — fragile
  under scope changes: if the rule broadens to "no two reservations at the
  same time anywhere," the state must move (to User?).

Conclusion: assigning state to individuals is a fool's errand; the premise
that state and actions naturally belong to individuals is mistaken.

## 8. Impacts on Modularity (summary)

Two key modularity failures: **fragmentation** (functions that belong together
scattered across modules → coupling) and **conflation** (unrelated functions
merged in one module → prevents reuse). In the example: assigning `reserve`
and its supporting relations to User conflates reservation logic with
authentication, karma tracking, notification preferences, reviews, etc. — the
User object becomes a fingerprint of the whole system, defeating reuse.
Maintaining both invariants (one reservation per slot; one reservation per
user per time) plus the constructor requirement fragments `reserve` across
three classes (Slot, User, Reservation), each with associated relations, and
the two invariants are split across objects; executing the action requires
coordinating three methods — fragmentation breeding coupling.

## 9. The problem of navigation (summary)

The intended direction of runtime navigation often dictates where relations
are assigned, producing dependencies opposite to what modularity would
suggest. Example: reviews. The natural home of the review-restaurant relation
is Review (each review targets exactly one restaurant), but the need to
display a restaurant's reviews pushes programmers to put a set of reviews in
Restaurant — making Restaurant depend on Review (even calling its
constructor). Judged against Parnas's four criteria for when module A may
"use" module B, this dependency fails — most clearly the fourth criterion,
since a useful subset with restaurants but no reviews obviously should exist.
This is a common pattern, not an outlier: natural navigation produces
dependencies in exactly the wrong direction.

## 10. Prior Mitigations (summary)

Surveys decades of workarounds: Meyer's analysis/design/implementation class
categories; "collection" objects (e.g., a `Reservations` class); DDD
**aggregates** that contain associated objects and preserve cross-object
invariants; multiple inheritance and mixins (largely abandoned);
subject-oriented programming and hyperslices; the entity-component system;
aspect-oriented programming (focused on cross-cutting concerns rather than
undoing conflation within objects); role-oriented programming and the DCI
framework (roles delegating to domain objects treated almost as a low-level
database); first-class relationships (RelJ). Design patterns mitigate
dependencies via abstract interfaces and indirection, but make code far harder
to understand by weakening the link between code-level computation and
problem-domain actions; the paper quotes the design-patterns book's own
observation that run-time structure bears little resemblance to code structure,
and notes Trygve Reenskaug called this "a frightening observation."

## 11. The Wider Impact of Object Orientation (summary)

Even systems not written as traditional OO programs inherit the problems:
object-relational mappers re-impose object structure on relational data. The
RealWorld benchmark (the Conduit microblogging app, implemented hundreds of
times as "best practice") shows the same assignment problems at coarser
granularity: layered (routing/controller/model) code divided into
vertical stacks per individual (User, Article, Comment) exhibits conflation
and fragmentation, with couplings across stacks — e.g., in one submission,
favoriting an article threads through the Article controller and model layers
*and* the User model layer, with a commented-out dependency in the reverse
direction evidencing the developer's struggle (paper's footnote 1).

## 12. A Simpler Approach to Modularity (summary)

Instead of assigning functionality to domain individuals, introduce a new
category of modules: **concepts**. Each concept embodies some actions and some
relations over the individuals participating in those actions. Concepts
partition the actions and relations of a behavior but **not** the individuals:
one individual can participate in multiple concepts.

Two features enable stronger modularity:

1. An individual can be mapped by relations in different concepts — an object
   is effectively split into distinct views (e.g., `UserAuthentication` maps
   User to passwords; `UserProfiling` maps the same User to display names and
   bios), preventing conflation.
2. **Concepts never call each other.** No delegation, no "using" another
   concept; each stands alone. Coordination happens by *synchronizing actions*
   with declarative rules establishing causal links.

The most fundamental OO idea survives: domain individuals are reified as
objects with persistent identity — but actions and state go to larger modules
spanning collections of objects. Since a concept can store sets of objects,
no collection classes are needed. Concepts can be implemented in any language
(in an OO language, a concept is a class instantiated for its state — a
"tiny machine"; multiple instances possible, e.g., for sharding). Concepts
resemble microservices (finer-grained) and modular monoliths; action
synchronization resembles event-driven architecture (sync rules playing a role
similar to DDD's anti-corruption layer) — but with a single category of
actions serving as API functions, bus events, and domain events at once, for
legibility.

## 13. An Example (specification figures quoted; commentary summarized)

Figure 1: The Reserving concept

```
concept Reserving [ User, Slot ]

purpose manage commitments to users for subsequent access to resources

principle
  after a user reserves a slot for a given date and time and party size,
  then if they turn up at that date and time with a party of that size
  they will be granted access to the slot

state
  a set of Reservations with
    a User
    a Slot
    a Time
    a Date
    a partySize Number
    a canceled Flag
    a granted Flag

actions
  reserve (user: User, slot: Slot, time: Time, date: Date, partySize: Number)
    : (reservation: Reservation)
    requires no existing reservation for slot,
      or for user on date and at a time close to time
    ensures creates a new reservation with canceled and granted false

  cancel (reservation: Reservation)
    requires reservation exists and is not canceled or granted
    ensures marks r as canceled

  grant (reservation: Reservation)
    requires reservation exists and is not canceled,
      and the current time and date is close to reservation’s time and date
    ensures marks reservation as granted

  noShow (reservation: Reservation)
    requires reservation exists and is not canceled or granted,
      and the current time and date is after reservation’s time and date,
    ensures marks reservation as canceled
```

Figure 2: The Availability concept

```
concept Availability [ Venue ]

purpose manage availability of resource slots at different venues

principle
  after some slots have been created for a venue at different times,
  one can get the available slots, and then toggle the availability of a slot
  to indicate that it has been assigned externally

state
  a set of Slots with
    a Venue
    a start Time
    an end Time
    a Date
    a minParty Number
    a maxParty Number
    an available Flag

actions
  create (venue: Venue, start: Time, end: Time, date: Date, max, min: Number): (slot: Slot)
    requires end is after start, max >= min
    ensures creates a new available slot with given properties

  toggleAvailable (slot: Slot)
    ensures toggles the available flag for slot

  _getAvailableSlot (time: Time, date: Date, partySize: Number): (slot: Slot)
    returns any available slot with start time matching time,
    and date matching given date and min <= partySize <= max
```

Figure 3: Sample synchronizations

```
sync makeReservation
when Requesting.reserve (restaurant, time, date, partySize, session)
where
  Availability._getAvailableSlot (time, date, partySize) : (slot)
  UserAuthentication._getUser (session) : (user)
  Karma._inGoodStanding (user)
then
  Reserving.reserve (user, slot, time, date, partySize)

sync punishNoShow
when Reserving.noShow (reservation)
where reservation.user = user in Reserving
then Karma.punish (user)
```

**Commentary (summarized):**

- Additional concepts are implied but not shown: `UserAuthentication`
  (session tokens; query from session to user), `Karma` (tracks user
  behavior), and a `RestaurantCatalog` (generates restaurant individuals).
- **Reserving vs. Availability:** Reserving is about *commitments* to users
  for later resource access; Availability is about *predicting* availability
  from resource allocation. Neither is certain — actual allocation (assigning
  reservations to tables) is a separate concept; slots don't map simply to
  tables (reconfiguration, overbooking).
- **Footnote 2 (cross-concept invariants):** one might expect an invariant
  that a reservation's time in Reserving matches its slot's start time in
  Availability, but in practice these can diverge (e.g., a diner calls to say
  they'll be late) and *mean different things*: the former is a commitment to
  a customer, the latter a prediction of availability. This explains why the
  expected cross-concept invariant does not hold.
- **The Requesting pseudo-concept and the abandoned CSP-style semantics:** in
  an earlier semantics for synchronizations (in *The Essence of Software*), a
  sync was a symmetric constraint on all its actions, following CSP: a
  requested action and (say) the granting of permission were inseparable — if
  either failed, neither happened. Designers found this hard to understand
  (intuitively, a request occurs and the action can then fail, but under that
  semantics a failed action means the request never occurred), hard to
  implement (it requires transactions), and it provided no simple way to log
  unsuccessful requests. The approach used here is simpler: synchronizations
  are causal rules — when one action happens, another may follow. Requests are
  reified as actions in their own right, so a request can occur without the
  action following; request and action can also carry different arguments
  (e.g., a request carries only a session token which `UserAuthentication`
  translates into a user). All user requests go in a pseudo-concept called
  `Requesting`, which in the implementation encapsulates HTTP and routing.
- **Concept anatomy:** name (with type parameters treated as type variables,
  e.g. `Availability [ Venue ]`); purpose (as specific as possible);
  operational principle (archetypal scenario showing how behavior fulfills the
  purpose); state (relations declared around individual types for
  succinctness — implying no object structuring; relation names explicit or
  implicit); actions in precondition/postcondition form, where preconditions
  are *firing conditions* (if false, the action may not occur) and results are
  named.
- **Queries:** the actions section can also specify queries, marked by an
  initial underscore. A query is not an action and cannot update state; it is
  a convenient way to specify a common state-reading pattern. A query always
  returns a set of bindings — `_getAvailableSlot` returns a set of slots
  (a singleton with an available slot, or empty if none exists). Concept state
  is assumed public, so no rich observer-style query set (as with abstract
  data types) is needed.
- Some timing details are deliberately underspecified (when a reservation
  counts as a no-show; how close in time two reservations may be — popular
  systems forbid two same-night reservations, which customers dislike).
- **Sync anatomy:** name (used in provenance tracking of causal links);
  `when` (pattern over triggering action occurrences; variables bound by name
  to action arguments, allowing partial matching); `where` (binds additional
  variables by querying concept state — e.g., resolving session to user,
  finding a matching slot, checking good standing); `then` (action executed
  for **every** binding the where clause produces — the paper's footnote 3
  notes this lets syncs express iteration, e.g., deleting all of a user's
  reservations when the user is deleted).
- **Contrasts with OO structure:** no composite objects (state is relations
  over individuals; no shared-mutable-object problems); no methods (actions
  have no single target; behavior ranges over whole concept state); no
  non-domain types (no collection classes needed); separation of concerns
  (the same individual, e.g. Slot, plays different roles in Reserving and
  Availability — and which side of a relation a type sits on is not fixed);
  externalized coordination (no inter-concept calls or references; all
  coordination in syncs, keeping concepts free-standing, reusable, less
  polluted by application specifics, and making causal links trackable);
  polymorphism (concepts are inherently unboundedly polymorphic — Reserving
  requires only that users and slots be distinguishable).

## 14. Conclusion (summary)

Concept structuring addresses the limitations of object orientation with a
clearer problem-domain-to-design correspondence and better separation of
concerns. Industrial experience has been positive; concepts serve as a shared
language across specialties (engineers and UX architects). Their independence
suits LLM-based code generation — specs and implementations can be generated
one concept at a time without other concepts in context. Challenges remain:
concepts are simple but not easy (designers are drawn to mechanism over
abstraction; disentangling concerns is hard). Behaviors that depend on
composite object structure (fingerprints/hashes, serialization, versioning)
are harder to capture, requiring parameterization over the relations that
comprise an object's contents. The essential idea might be called **relational
modularity**: where aspect-oriented programming and DDD's bounded contexts
sought modularity by scoping *objects*, concepts scope *relations and their
invariants*. All core elements existed in the 1990s (datalog, Z/B and view
specification, ER model, semantic networks), but the problems of that era were
storage, transactions, and query optimization; today — with fast hardware,
team-owned models, bounded contexts and ubiquitous event-driven architectures
— it may be time to reconsider a core assumption that has shaped programming
for decades.
